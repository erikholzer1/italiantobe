Why It Matters

More than a passport.

Italian citizenship is not a document you file away. It is a standing right to a different life, available to you the moment your claim is recognized, and to your children after you without them ever having to ask.

🇪🇺 Live anywhere in the EU

Settle in any of the 27 member states without a visa and without a sponsor. You move under EU free movement law instead of going through a country's immigration system.

💼 Work in 27 labor markets

No work permit in any member state, whether employed, self-employed, or opening your own company, with the same treatment as a national on pay, conditions, and registration. Licensing and tax rules still apply. Whether you're allowed to work or do business there does not.

🏥 Universal healthcare

As an Italian citizen, you can enroll in Italy's public healthcare system, ranked among the best in the world, typically for a modest annual contribution rather than private insurance premiums. EU rules extend reciprocal coverage when you live or travel elsewhere in the union.

🎓 Study at citizen rates

EU citizens are generally treated as domestic students rather than international ones, which can change the cost of a degree substantially. You can live in the country while you study, and work without a permit.

👨‍👩‍👧 Your children inherit it

Recognition can carry citizenship to your children, giving them the United States and the EU from birth. Transmission rules have tightened in recent years, so we look at the rules that apply at the time rather than assume.

💍 Your family comes with you

When you move to another member state, EU law gives your spouse and close family the right to join you, live there and work. That holds even when they are not EU citizens themselves.

Other Routes

Don't qualify by descent? Other paths still exist.

Law 74/2025 closed the automatic descent route for many, but it also created new options. Here are the legitimate alternatives worth knowing about.

Fastest for descendants

2-Year Residency, Italian Grandchild Route

Law 74/2025 actually created an accelerated path: if your parent or grandparent was an Italian citizen by birth, you can relocate to Italy, register at your municipality, and apply for naturalization after just 2 continuous years of legal residence, reduced from the previous 3.

This is a discretionary state grant, not an automatic right, and requires you to genuinely live in Italy. But for those who've been thinking about making the move anyway, it's a real option.

Standard route

Standard Residency Naturalization

Italy grants citizenship to anyone who has resided legally and continuously in the country for a set number of years: 10 years for non-EU nationals, 4 years for EU citizens, 5 years for stateless persons or recognized refugees.

No Italian ancestry required. This is the same path available to anyone who builds a life in Italy regardless of heritage.

Marriage route

Citizenship Through Marriage to an Italian

If you are married to an Italian citizen, you may apply for citizenship after 2 years of marriage while residing in Italy, or 3 years if residing abroad. These timeframes are reduced by half if the couple has children together.

This is a well-established route with its own document requirements, and a realistic option for those with an Italian spouse.

Common Questions

Things people ask before they start

The process raises a lot of questions. Here are the ones we hear most often.

How far back can my Italian ancestor be?

Under Italy's reformed citizenship law (Law 74/2025, effective May 2025), jure sanguinis is now limited to two generations: your parent or grandparent must be the Italian-born ancestor. Previously, there was no generational limit. Claims through great-grandparents or further are no longer automatically recognized for new applications under the updated law.

Do I have to give up my US citizenship?

No. The United States allows dual citizenship, and Italy actively grants it. You keep your US passport in full and gain an Italian one alongside it.

What if my lineage goes through a woman born before 1948?

This is known as a 1948 case. Italy's 1912 law didn't allow women to transmit citizenship, so if a female ancestor needed to pass the chain to a child born before 1948, that link was broken under the old rules. You can still claim citizenship, but it requires filing directly with an Italian court (an azione di stato), not the standard consular route. It's a longer process, but it absolutely works. Read the full breakdown, including how long it takes and where it's filed.

How long does the whole process take?

It varies widely, anywhere from several months to several years, depending on your consulate, your ancestral commune, and document availability. We help you set realistic expectations from the start.

Can I do this without moving to Italy?

Yes. The consulate route lets you apply through the Italian consulate in your home country. No move required, though an Italy visit can sometimes speed up the commune records step.

What is the "minor issue" and does it affect my case?

Under Italy's 1912 citizenship law, if an Italian ancestor voluntarily naturalized in a foreign country while their child was still a minor living with them, that minor child automatically lost Italian citizenship too, breaking the chain. Critically, before March 10, 1975, the Italian age of majority was 21, not 18. This means a "child" who was 19 or 20 at the time of the parent's naturalization may still be affected. This has now been settled, and favorably. In Sezioni Unite 24045/2026 (deposited July 26, 2026), Italy's Supreme Court held that a child who was a dual citizen at birth, Italian by descent and a citizen of their birth country by being born there, keeps Italian citizenship regardless of a parent naturalizing during their minority. On August 10, 2026, the Interior Ministry issued a circular directing every consulate and comune to apply the ruling, and instructing them to accept a request for re-examination of the original application rather than requiring applicants to file a new one from scratch. Read the full breakdown, including exactly who this affects.

What if I don't qualify by descent under the new rules?

Alternative pathways exist for those who don't qualify by descent under the new rules, including residency-based naturalization and citizenship through marriage. Eligibility and timelines vary. Take a Pre-Eligibility Assessment and book a call with us to discuss what may apply to your situation.
